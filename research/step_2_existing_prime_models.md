# Step 2 — Existing models of prime behaviour

Date: 2026-11-26  
Project: The Prime Pattern  
Status: Model-literacy note; no new experiment approved by this note.

## Purpose

Before proposing a new model or experiment, distinguish established theorems, conjectural predictions, heuristic stochastic models, and conditional randomization controls. These are not interchangeable.

## 1. Prime Number Theorem (PNT)

For the prime-counting function \(\pi(x)\),

\[
\pi(x)\sim \frac{x}{\log x}.
\]

The PNT describes average density at large scales. It does not specify exact prime locations or, by itself, the full variance and covariance of prime counts in short fixed intervals.

## 2. Cramér's random model

A basic Cramér-style heuristic treats integers near \(n\) as independent prime candidates with probability approximately \(1/\log n\), with small exceptional cases handled separately.

It is useful as a simple baseline for prime gaps and irregularity. Its independence assumption ignores the deterministic divisibility restrictions imposed by small primes, so it is not an adequate literal model of actual primes at fine scales.

Cramér's conjecture about maximal prime gaps is a separate conjectural statement; it should not be confused with the random model itself.

## 3. Granville's refinement

Granville highlighted the need to account for small-prime divisibility before applying an independent thinning idea. This addresses an important defect in the naive Cramér model: actual primes cannot be multiples of small primes.

A finite-sieve-plus-thinning construction, like the one used in our project, is inspired by this general idea. It is not automatically identical to every formulation of Granville's model, and it does not automatically capture all prime correlations.

## 4. Hardy–Littlewood prime-tuple conjectures

Hardy–Littlewood predicts asymptotic counts of prime tuples using an arithmetic singular series. For prime pairs, the predicted rate depends on the gap and on local divisibility obstructions.

This is a conjectural description of actual prime correlations, not a random simulation procedure. It is a key theoretical benchmark for understanding why prime candidates are not independent.

## 5. Banks–Ford–Tao random sieve model

Banks, Ford and Tao introduced a more sophisticated random sieve model using independently chosen forbidden residue classes modulo small primes, with a cutoff that changes with scale. The model is designed to reproduce Hardy–Littlewood-type singular-series behaviour in its random setting.

Our finite model that fixes the forbidden residue to zero for primes dividing the block length is a conditioned/adapted construction. It should not be called the original BFT model or treated as a theorem about actual primes.

## 6. Conditional permutation controls

The modulo-30 and modulo-210 shuffles used in earlier experiments are not full prime models. They are conditional randomization controls: they preserve selected observed block-count distributions within residue phases while randomizing order.

They answer a narrower question: does the observed ordering statistic look unusual among sequences satisfying those particular preserved constraints?

They do not establish that the shuffled sequences are realistic samples of prime counts.

## 7. Comparison for our project

| Approach | What it captures | What it does not automatically capture |
|---|---|---|
| PNT | Average density | Local covariance and exact positions |
| Cramér | Basic independent randomness and gap heuristics | Small-prime divisibility and prime-pair correlations |
| Granville-inspired sieve/thinning | Small-prime restrictions plus residual randomness | All higher-order prime correlations |
| Hardy–Littlewood | Conjectural prime-tuple frequencies and singular-series factors | A complete finite-sample probability law for our block statistic |
| BFT random sieve | A richer random model designed to match tuple singular-series behaviour | A proof that actual primes are generated by this random process |
| Phase-stratified permutation | Conditional ordering benchmark | A generative model of prime numbers |

## 8. Lessons from our experiments

- Experiments 23B and 24 were broadly compatible with the specified finite z=613 model for their tested statistics.
- Experiment 25 showed a notable discrepancy for block length 210, lag 3, in one independent interval.
- One discrepant interval is evidence against that particular model/statistic combination in that interval; it is not evidence of a new prime law.
- The model cutoff, conditioning, thinning, interval alignment, and sampling variation all matter.
- A future comparison must state precisely which model is being tested and must not change its parameters after seeing the test data.

We should be able to explain:
1. Why the PNT is a density theorem, not an exact predictor.
2. Why the naive Cramér model is too simple for fine arithmetic structure.

5. Why a conditional permutation test is not a complete prime model.
